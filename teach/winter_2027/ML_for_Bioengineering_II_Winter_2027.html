Three kinds of biomedical data, one skill set

In ML I you learned the core ideas. In ML II you take them to the data bioengineers actually work with, using the deep learning methods behind today's medical AI.

Images

Classify and segment

Use CNNs and pretrained models to find cells in microscopy and outline the heart in echocardiograms.

Signals

Model time series

Analyze ECG and wearable data with recurrent networks, and learn why many published models fail on new patients.

Literature

Build a research assistant

Use embeddings and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to answer questions from papers and protocols, with citations you can check.

What you'll build

Three hands-on demos, each paired with a written homework, building toward a final project you can show in a research interview or on your portfolio. The demo shows you can build it; the homework shows you understand why it works.

  1. Due week 3

    Demo 1: Train and compare

    Train a neural network on biomedical data and show whether it beats a simpler baseline.

    Written HW 1

    Explain your regularization choices, interpret Dice and IoU, and justify patient-level splits.

  2. Due week 5

    Demo 2: Model and validate

    Model images or signals, then audit your model for data leakage and test how well it generalizes.

    Written HW 2

    Spot leakage in study designs, read calibration results, and explain why models fail at new sites.

  3. Due week 8

    Demo 3: Retrieve and ground

    Build a working RAG pipeline over biomedical documents and check that its answers match the sources.

    Written HW 3

    Explain how attention works, compare semantic and keyword search, and judge whether answers are supported.

  4. Week 10

    Final project

    Take on a real biomedical problem using deep learning, RAG, or both. Present it with a report.

    Written report

    Problem, data, method, evaluation, biological interpretation and limitations.

Each written homework is released and due together with its demo, the same format as ML I. Expect short-answer questions on why a method works, how to read its results and where it can fail. Dates are proposed and will be confirmed before the quarter starts.

Questions students ask

Do I need to have taken ML I?

No, it’s recommended but not required. Students who took ML for Bioengineering I will have the smoothest start, since ML II builds on its ideas and tools. If you haven’t, you’ll be fine with some familiarity with train/test splits and accuracy. Week 1 starts with a refresher on neural networks, and the ML I course page is a good resource for filling gaps.

I'm not a strong programmer. Will I keep up?

The course focuses on choosing the right method and judging the results, not memorizing code. You'll use AI coding assistants to write and debug code, and learn to check what they produce.

What is the written homework like?

Three short assignments, one per unit, each released and due with its demo. No coding: you explain why a method fits the data, interpret results like Dice scores or calibration, spot flaws such as data leakage, and judge whether a RAG answer is backed by its sources. It's where you show you understand what the AI-assisted code is doing.

Do I need a GPU or paid software?

No. Required work uses free and open-source tools, and Google Colab provides free GPU time. Free-tier limits can vary, so start assignments early.

Can I use data from my own lab?

Often, yes, for the final project, as long as you're authorized to use it and follow your lab's privacy rules. Talk with the instructor early.

How is the course graded?

Through three written homework assignments, the three demos, a final project report and a final presentation. Weights and exact dates are still being finalized.
